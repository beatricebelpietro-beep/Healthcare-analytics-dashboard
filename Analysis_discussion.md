# Healthcare Spending, National Wealth and Health Outcomes: A Cross-Country Analysis

This project investigates the relationship between healthcare expenditure, national wealth, and life expectancy across a sample of 36 countries using OECD and World Bank data for the period 2015–2024. The analysis focuses on three main indicators: healthcare expenditure as a percentage of GDP, GDP per capita, and life expectancy. The objective is not only to identify broad statistical relationships, but also to examine cross-country differences and understand why some countries appear to achieve stronger health outcomes than others despite similar or lower levels of financial resources.

A first important result concerns the relationship between national wealth and life expectancy. Among the variables considered, GDP per capita shows the strongest linear association with life expectancy, with an R² of approximately 0.347. This suggests that wealthier countries tend, on average, to experience higher life expectancy. The result is intuitive: higher levels of national income can support better infrastructure, nutrition, education, housing, healthcare access, and public services, all of which may contribute to improved health outcomes.

However, the relationship is far from deterministic. An R² of 0.347 means that a substantial share of cross-country variation in life expectancy remains unexplained by GDP per capita alone. Countries with similar levels of income can still display markedly different health outcomes. This indicates that national wealth should be understood as an important enabling factor rather than a sufficient explanation for population health.

The relationship between healthcare expenditure and life expectancy is considerably weaker. Healthcare expenditure as a share of GDP has an R² of approximately 0.155 with life expectancy. This means that countries allocating a larger share of their national income to healthcare do not necessarily achieve proportionally better health outcomes. The result highlights an important distinction between the amount of resources devoted to healthcare and the effectiveness with which those resources are used.

The United States represents the clearest example of this pattern. It records the highest average healthcare expenditure in the sample, at approximately 17.05% of GDP, while its average life expectancy is around 78.18 years, below the sample average. Relative to its exceptionally high level of healthcare spending, the United States therefore appears to underperform in terms of life expectancy. This does not imply that healthcare expenditure itself reduces health outcomes. Rather, it suggests that high spending alone cannot guarantee superior population health and that the organization, accessibility, efficiency, and broader social context of the healthcare system may be equally important.

Japan provides an almost opposite example. It records the highest average life expectancy in the sample, approximately 84.23 years, while healthcare expenditure is high but far below the level observed in the United States. Moreover, Japan does not have the highest GDP per capita in the sample. Its performance therefore suggests that high life expectancy can be achieved without being either the richest country or the largest healthcare spender.

A similar pattern emerges in several European countries. Italy and Spain, for example, combine relatively high life expectancy with more moderate levels of GDP per capita and healthcare expenditure. Italy records an average life expectancy of approximately 82.93 years while remaining below the sample average in both GDP per capita and healthcare expenditure. Spain shows a comparable pattern, with life expectancy of approximately 83.16 years. These cases indicate that financial resources matter, but that their relationship with health outcomes is mediated by other factors.

The quadrant analysis reinforces this conclusion. A group of countries, including Greece, Iceland, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Luxembourg, and Slovenia, achieves above-average life expectancy while remaining below the sample average in healthcare expenditure as a percentage of GDP. These countries can be classified as “Low Spending – High Outcomes” within the descriptive framework of the project.

However, this group is itself heterogeneous. Countries such as Luxembourg, Ireland, and Iceland have relatively high levels of GDP per capita, while Greece, Italy, and Slovenia achieve high life expectancy despite having income levels below the sample average. The latter cases are particularly interesting because their outcomes cannot be explained simply by high national wealth. They suggest that healthcare system design, prevention, social conditions, diet, lifestyle, and other institutional or cultural factors may contribute significantly to population health.

The analysis also reveals that GDP per capita is only weakly associated with healthcare expenditure as a percentage of GDP, with an R² of approximately 0.073. This is an important finding because it shows that richer countries do not necessarily devote a larger share of their economy to healthcare. A country may have a very high GDP per capita while spending a relatively modest percentage of GDP on healthcare, yet still spend substantially more in absolute terms per person than a poorer country.

This distinction is essential when interpreting the results. Healthcare expenditure in this project is measured as a percentage of GDP rather than as expenditure per capita. Therefore, a lower percentage does not necessarily imply lower absolute healthcare resources. It instead reflects how large a share of national income is allocated to healthcare.

The analysis also suggests the presence of diminishing returns at high levels of income. Luxembourg, for example, has the highest GDP per capita in the sample and high life expectancy, but its health outcomes are not proportionally higher than those of countries with much lower income levels. Once countries reach a certain level of economic development, additional income may generate progressively smaller gains in life expectancy.

Overall, the findings suggest that health outcomes depend on a broader combination of economic, institutional, social, and behavioral factors. National wealth appears to create favorable conditions for better health, but wealth alone does not determine life expectancy. Similarly, allocating a larger share of GDP to healthcare does not automatically translate into superior outcomes.

The central conclusion of the project is therefore that healthcare performance should not be evaluated exclusively through the amount of money spent. The way resources are organized and transformed into prevention, access, treatment, and population-level health improvements may be just as important as the level of expenditure itself.

Countries such as Japan, Italy, and Spain illustrate how strong outcomes can coexist with more moderate levels of financial resources, while the United States demonstrates that exceptionally high healthcare expenditure does not necessarily produce proportionally better results. These differences highlight the importance of looking beyond aggregate spending and considering the broader economic and institutional environment in which healthcare systems operate.

Finally, the analysis should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal. The observed relationships identify cross-country associations, but they do not demonstrate that GDP or healthcare expenditure directly causes changes in life expectancy. Further analysis incorporating variables such as inequality, education, demographic structure, obesity, smoking, healthcare access, prevention, and institutional quality would be necessary to develop a more complete explanation of the differences observed across countries.
